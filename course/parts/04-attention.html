Attention is the most visible part of a transformer: for each head, the weights are just a grid of numbers you can look at directly. That makes attention heatmaps the natural first tool. This lesson teaches you to read them — and to find a famous circuit hiding in ours.

How to read a heatmap

A heatmap shows one head on one input. Each row is a query (a position doing the looking) and each column is a key (a position being looked at); a dark cell means that query put a lot of attention on that key. Because of the causal mask, everything is lower-triangular — a position can only look backward.

Already you can see the heads are specialized — they do not all look the same. Two of them (L0H0, L0H1) show a clean diagonal in the input region: each token attending to itself or its neighbour. One (L1H1) is broad. And one (L1H0) is sharp but its bright cells are scattered in a way that looks almost random. That last one is the interesting one.

Finding the induction head

Zoom in on L1H0 and organize the picture. At each step where the model is about to emit a value, where does that position look?

Now it is not random at all. When the model is about to emit the value that belongs with the smallest-key pair, this head attends precisely to that value in the input, and copies it. It is doing the routing the whole task is about.

Definition

An induction head is a head that implements "find X earlier in the context, and copy what came after it." Induction heads were first discovered in language models (where they drive in-context learning) and are the circuit transformers form most readily. Here, X is a key and "what came after it" is that key's value.

This is a genuine, named circuit — the kind of thing interpretability exists to find — and we found it by looking at pictures and then organizing them by what the model was doing at each step. That reorganization ("sort the columns by rank") is a recurring trick: raw attention can look like noise until you view it in the right coordinates.

Heads have jobs: an atlas

Reading all four heads together, a division of labour emerges. It helps to name each head by its role:

  • L1H0 — the induction / value head: copies each value from its source pair (the scattered-but-sharp map above).
  • L1H1 — the sort head: reads the keys emitted so far to decide the next one (the broad map).
  • L0H0, L0H1 — precursor heads: the diagonal "previous-token" heads whose output the later heads build on. They also do something clever at the SEP token that we will only fully understand with causal tools.

The catch: attention is only a suggestion

Everything above is observation. A heatmap tells you where a head looks — but not whether that looking actually matters for the output, nor what the head does with what it reads. A head can attend somewhere and have its contribution cancelled downstream; another can look diffuse but be essential.

Why we cannot stop here

Attention maps are descriptive, not causal. "This head looks like an induction head" is a hypothesis. To promote it to a fact we have to intervene — delete the head and check that value-copying breaks, and only value-copying. That is the subject of the next lesson, and it is where interpretability gets its teeth.
